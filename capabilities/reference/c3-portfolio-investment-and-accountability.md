# C3. Portfolio, Investment and Accountability

## Capability summary

| Capability ID | Function | Purpose | Primary owner | Primary standard | Key outputs | Key evidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| C3 | DIRECT | Make explicit lifecycle and funding decisions. | Portfolio decision-maker | ODPC and ODPG | Product decision and accountable owner | Approval, funding and review record |

## Purpose

C3 decides whether to create, reuse, extend, merge, maintain, deprecate or retire a product, and who remains accountable for its outcome and operating cost.

## Why it matters

An inventory is not a portfolio. Without explicit decisions, duplicate offerings, unfunded operations and unowned products accumulate.

## When it applies

Apply it after demand qualification and at material lifecycle events: investment, major change, persistent assurance failure, loss of demand or retirement.

## Inputs

Use case, objective, reuse search, gap analysis, expected value, operating cost, risk, dependencies and ownership options are inputs.

## Practices

Compare options, select a lifecycle decision, assign an accountable product owner, identify build and operating funding, and set a review trigger. Record what evidence and assumptions informed the decision.

## Roles and accountability

The portfolio decision-maker is accountable for investment; the product owner is accountable for the product outcome; finance, governance and platform roles advise or approve within their authority.

## Outputs

Produce a decision record, owner, funding source, priority, lifecycle state, dependency view and review date.

## ODPS family mapping

ODPC holds product references and portfolio context; ODPG connects decisions to use cases, objectives and dependencies. ODPS identifies the resulting individual product only after the decision.

## Evidence

Retain options considered, approval, funding confirmation, ownership acceptance, review history and continuation or retirement decision.

## Measures

Measure active products with owners and operating funding, overlapping propositions, products without active demand, review timeliness and avoided duplication.

## Example

The customer portfolio board decides to extend Customer 360 with service interactions instead of creating a churn-only duplicate. It funds the extension, names the customer-data product owner and requires a six-month value review.

## Questions to ask

- Why are we funding this product now?
- Which use cases justify it?
- Who pays to build and operate it?
- Does an existing product satisfy the demand?
- Who can make lifecycle decisions?
- What would cause us to stop investing?

## Common failure modes

Funding delivery but not operations, assigning a delivery manager instead of an accountable owner, and retaining products because no review date exists are common failures.

## Minimum implementation

Use a documented create, reuse or extend decision with one accountable owner, operating funding and a review date.

## Advanced implementation

Use portfolio evidence to compare product overlap, dependency concentration, cost, adoption and outcome across domains.

## AI-Agent-First extension

Agents can surface overlap or stale evidence; human authority remains required for investment, risk acceptance and retirement.

## Assessment levels 1-5

Level 1 relies on project approval. Level 2 defines ownership and lifecycle decisions. Level 3 retains decision evidence. Level 4 measures portfolio health and exceptions. Level 5 adapts investments using outcome and assurance evidence.
